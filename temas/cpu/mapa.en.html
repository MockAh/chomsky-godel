X-ray of
a computer

Seen from the inside, a computer splits into two distinct tissues. One computes and remembers nothing: its output depends only on what comes in right now. The other remembers and does not compute: it holds the result until the next clock pulse. The whole machine is the back and forth between the two. This color code is the X-ray.

Combinational tissue

No memory. Same inputs, same output, always. It is a Boolean algebra, and therefore a formula of propositional logic.

Stateful tissue

It remembers. The output depends on the history, not on the current inputs. It lies outside the algebra: no formula describes it.

Nine parts, two tissues

What we call a "processor" is this circuit, traversed from left to right once per cycle. The amber blocks hold values from one cycle to the next; the cyan blocks transform them. Four of them —the ALU, the multiplexers, the decoder and the control logic— have a dotted outline: they are the computing heart of the machine.

Start wherever you like. Each part says what it does, which tissue it belongs to, and why that distinction matters.

In the workshop: the notebook and the reading finger

Two parts of the register file, seen from the inside with a nine-step program. rsi holds a shelf number, not the piece itself: only the brackets [rsi] go to the storeroom and bring the piece to the workbench. And rflags is the notebook where the ALU writes down how its last calculation went: cmp, sub, add and inc write it; mov and jumps leave it alone, and je only reads it.

    Where the isomorphism lives

    If you isolate the cyan tissue, what remains is not a simplification of the computer: it is a closed mathematical object. Combinational circuits with n inputs form a finite Boolean algebra, the same one formed by propositional formulas in n variables once those that are provably equivalent are identified.

    Φ/~ ≅ ℘(X) ≅ {Boolean functions of n inputs} with |X| = 2ⁿ

    Read from left to right: formulas modulo provable equivalence, sets of valuations —the true rows of a truth table— and memoryless circuits. Three presentations of a single thing. That is why the ALU does not imitate propositional reasoning: it realizes it.

    Amber is the limit. A register is not a Boolean function of its inputs, so it is not an element of that algebra —it is not that it lacks a preimage: it is outside the setting. The map from the algebra into the whole computer is injective as a function of sets; only when the codomain is restricted to the combinational tissue does it become an isomorphism.
