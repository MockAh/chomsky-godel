From connective to copper · combinational logic

A logical connective is
a way of letting current through

There is no translation or analogy here: there is structural identity. If you agree that a high voltage means 1 and a low one means 0, then a two-input circuit is already computing a Boolean function — whether you like it or not. Each of the three operations of the algebra has its own physical gesture, and they are very simple gestures: put in a row, put in parallel, invert.

1 · HIGH

About 5 V or 3.3 V in old or hobby circuits; close to 1 V inside a modern CPU. It is called VDD. In the algebra: [⊤], true.

0 · LOW

0 V, ground. In the algebra: [⊥], false.

In between

Forbidden. The circuit is designed to cross that zone quickly and not linger: that is why noise does not corrupt the value.

Inputs

One single state, three depths: everything below responds to these two switches.

∧

Conjunction · two obstacles in a row

output 0

For current to reach the lamp it must pass through both switches, one after the other. One open switch is enough to break the path. That is exactly what the table of p ∧ q says: a single 0 ruins the result.

Level 2 · real CMOS

In silicon the natural gate is NAND: four transistors. AND costs two more, because the result has to be inverted.

∨

Disjunction · two alternative paths

output 0

Now the switches do not get in each other's way: each has its own route to the lamp. If either one closes, current flows. Closing both does not make it brighter — the current was already getting through, and that is why 1 ∨ 1 = 1 and not 2. This shows why disjunction looks like a sum but is not one.

Level 1 · switches in parallel

Two switches for the same light, each with its own wire. (Careful: two-way staircase switches are not this; those compute p ⊕ q, the XOR.)

Level 2 · real CMOS

The natural gate here is NOR: the PMOS are in series and the NMOS in parallel — the exact mirror of the NAND. De Morgan, cast in silicon.

Transistors: 6 — 4 for the NOR + 2 for the inverter.

Level 3 · symbol and table

The curved tip of the symbol tells OR apart from AND at a glance.

¬

Negation · the contact that opens when energized

output 1

Negation is different from the other two: it does not combine, it transforms. And it needs something the previous ones did not: an element that reacts to a signal — a relay, a transistor. A wire cannot negate anything. Here the coil of p, when energized, pulls the contact and opens it: the lamp goes off exactly when the input goes high.

Level 1 · normally closed relay

At rest the contact is closed and the lamp is on. Activating p turns it off.

Level 2 · real CMOS

The inverter: two complementary transistors. When one conducts, the other is off. At rest there is never a direct path from VDD to ground —only for an instant, while switching—: that is why it draws almost no power while it is not changing.

Transistors: 2 — the cheapest gate there is.

The little circle at the tip is the negation. It appears attached to other gates to form NAND and NOR.

What you just saw

Three facts the circuit teaches and the table hides

Series and parallel

The ∧ / ∨ duality is a topological duality

In Boolean algebra, laws come in dual pairs. Here you can see where that symmetry comes from: swapping ∧ for ∨ is swapping “in a row” for “in parallel”. Those are the only two ways of connecting two things.

Asymmetric cost

Negating is cheap; affirming is expensive

CMOS only produces inverted gates naturally: NAND and NOR cost 4 transistors, and AND and OR cost 6 because the inversion has to be undone. In the algebra all four are equally simple; in silicon they are not.

Functional completeness

NAND alone is enough

From ¬p = p NAND p and p ∧ q = ¬(p NAND q) everything else follows. In principle, an entire chip could be built from a single repeated piece; in practice a library of several cells is used because it is cheaper, but none of them adds computing power.

The exact statement

Why this is not an analogy

A combinational circuit with k inputs defines a function {0,1}ᵏ → {0,1}. The set of all those functions, with gate-by-gate operations, is a finite Boolean algebra with 2^(2ᵏ) elements: it is ℘(X), taking X as the set of the 2ᵏ input combinations (each function is given by the rows where it is 1). And it is the same algebra Lindenbaum–Tarski builds from formulas in k variables, Φ/∼.

Φ/∼  ≅  ℘(X)  ≅  combinational circuits with k inputs

With two variables, that makes 16 essentially different circuits — not one more. Any tangle of two-input gates you build, however convoluted, is equivalent to one of those 16. The isomorphism does not allow a seventeenth to exist.

The fine print

It only holds for the memoryless part

Everything on this page is combinational: the output depends only on the current inputs. The ALU, the multiplexers and the decoders live here. Registers, cache, RAM and the program counter do not: they have state, and a logical formula has no way of remembering anything.

The next step

Closing the loop changes category

Feeding the output of these same gates back into their input is enough to get a flip-flop, and with it a finite-state machine. That loop is the border: on the other side there is no Boolean algebra anymore, there are automata.

The algebra does not describe the circuit: the circuit is a realization of the algebra. Changing from copper to silicon, or to relays, or to jets of water in pipes, does not change the mathematical object — only the material in which it is asked to appear.
