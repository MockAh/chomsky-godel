§ 1.1The three tools

Going from paper to the machine takes three pieces, plus checking that the processor really is x86-64, the architecture that was studied.

  • nasm the assembler: translates the sheet written in words (mov rax, 60) into machine bytes.
  • ld the linker (it comes in binutils): packs those bytes into an executable.
  • gdb the debugger: stops the CPU between one line and the next to look at the registers.

§ 2.1Ending a program

On paper the sheet «ended» by itself. On a real machine there is no end of sheet: if the finger (rip) keeps going down, it reads garbage and the CPU stops with SIGSEGV. The hardware does not recover by itself.

To finish, the workshop knocks on the owner's door (the operating system) with syscall. The owner reads two pieces from the bench:

  • rax the request number. 60 = «close the workshop». It is not free to choose.
  • rdi the request's data: the exit code. It is free; by convention, 0 = all good.
In your language syscall is evaluating a function that is not in your sheet but in the kernel; the registers are its arguments.

§ 2.2Sheet, bench, notebook, finger

The central confusion of that session was where each thing happens. They are four separate objects:

ObjectWhat it containsIn the machine
The sheetOnly orders, line by line. Nothing is written on it..text, in the storeroom
The benchWhere the Carpenter does the calculationsRegisters (rax, rdi…)
The notebookOnly marks about the last calculationrflags (ZF…)
The fingerWhich line we are onrip

Questions you asked

What is section .text?
The area of the storeroom where the instruction sheet goes. .data = pieces with an initial value (used in mini-task 5). Sheet and pieces in the same storeroom, on different shelves: von Neumann.
What are _start: and global _start?
_start: is a label, a note in the margin: «it starts here». global makes it visible so that ld knows where to put the finger at startup.
Is the OS the Carpenter?
No. Carpenter + Master Builder = CPU. The OS is the owner of the workshop: it decides which sheet comes in, assigns shelves, puts the finger on _start and, with syscall, closes the workshop.
Does the notebook store the result?
No: the result stays on the bench (sub rax, rbx leaves the 7 in rax); the notebook only keeps marks about it: ZF (was it zero?), SF (negative?), CF (carry?), OF (signed overflow?).

§ 3.1Each path ends on its own

On paper, the sheet ended after je. On the machine, every path must end by knocking on the owner's door; otherwise, the finger drops into whatever follows. The exit code is the witness of which path the CPU took, with no debugger: jumps → 1, does not jump → 0.

§ 3.2Worked example: A.1 in real code

global _start
section .text
_start:
    mov  rax, 10
    mov  rbx, 3
    sub  rax, rbx       ; rax = 7
    cmp  rax, 7         ; 7 − 7 = 0 → ZF = 1
    je   equal          ; jumps

    mov  rax, 60        ; «did not jump» path
    mov  rdi, 0
    syscall

equal:                  ; «jumped» path
    mov  rax, 60
    mov  rdi, 1
    syscall
The trap If the «did not jump» path were missing its syscall, the finger would fall into equal: and it would exit with 1 in both cases: the witness would no longer tell them apart. A block with no jump and no syscall spills over into the next one.

§ 4.3What gdb confirmed

  • mov does not write in the notebook: eflags does not change in steps 1 and 2.
  • je only reads: after the jump, rax and eflags stay the same.
  • a label is a number: gdb shows je 0x40101f <equal>; the name is a courtesy.
The brackets in $eflags
The notebook as a function

The notebook is f : {marks} → {0,1}, and gdb shows its support f⁻¹(1): in [ PF ZF IF ] are the marks that are 1; the ones that do not appear are 0.

IF
The owner's mark: no calculation sets it, the OS does («the workshop accepts interrupts»). Always on.
AF after sub
There was a borrow in the lower 4 bits: 20 = 0001 0100, 6 = 0000 0110; 4 − 6 does not reach.
PF after cmp
The low byte of the result (0) has an even number of ones. AF and PF are old marks, hardly used.

§ 5.1Pieces with their own address

section .data
pieces: db 5, 3, 8, 2     ; 4 consecutive bytes
  • pieces: a note in the margin; nasm replaces it with the number of the first shelf.
  • mov rsi, pieces puts the shelf number on the bench, not the piece.
  • mov al, [rsi] goes to the shelf and brings the piece to al (the low byte of rax).
  • inc rsi moves forward one shelf. It touches no piece.
In your language It is the sequence a : {0,1,2,3} → bytes: rsi runs through the domain and [rsi] evaluates the function. Your F(F(x)) from A.2, with a finger that moves.

The neighbouring shelf is not yours
